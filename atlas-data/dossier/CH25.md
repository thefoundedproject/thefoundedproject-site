# CH25 — Assets, Liabilities, and Honest Accounting

*Part IV: Governing Agency · Stage: governance · Draft: Draft 1.2 — Voice rewrite 2026-08-23; plain-language sweep 2026-08-24; structural voice rewrite 2026-08-28; direct address conversion (second person) 2026-08-30; plain-abstraction recheck 2026-09-01 (2026-08-23) · 2186 words*

## Thesis (Overview › Core Claim)

A regulated enterprise knows what it owns and what it owes. Most adults cannot describe their own enterprise with comparable accuracy, financially or otherwise, and honest accounting across all seven capital forms is the foundation the governance work in the rest of Part IV depends on.

## Five-minute summary (Overview text)

**Purpose.** Takes the seven capitals CH24 defined and turns them into a practice. Where CH24 named the forms of wealth an enterprise holds, CH25 walks the specific inventory that makes those forms visible: a three-column, seven-row accounting that surfaces what the reader actually owns, owes, and could develop, across financial and non-financial capital alike.

**Reader problem.** The reader is carrying financial and non-financial debts they have not fully named, and financial and non-financial assets they have not fully claimed. The chapter gives the reader the specific inventory practice that makes both visible, rather than another exhortation to "get organized."

**Movement.** Five sections: "Why Most Adults Cannot Describe What They Have" (the diagnosis, with the ostrich-effect citation) → "What the Inventory Requires" (the three-column, seven-row structure walked row by row) → "What Honest Accounting Specifically Means" (naming the two distortions, minimized debts and under-claimed assets) → "What the Inventory Allows" (the decisions an honest inventory makes possible) → "What the Next Chapter Walks" (hand-off to the personal board in CH26, and the Journey from the Edge cross-reference).

**Takeaway.** The reader closes the chapter with the actual tool, the three-column, seven-row inventory, and with permission to look honestly at both what they owe and what they have refused to claim, without either becoming an occasion for self-punishment.

## Key concepts

- three-column inventory: assets, liabilities, developable capacities
- seven capital rows, inherited directly from CH24: financial, time, attention, health, relational, knowledge, reputational
- the ostrich effect as the documented mechanism behind incomplete self-knowledge of financial liabilities specifically
- two distortions honest accounting corrects: minimizing debts and under-claiming assets, which run in opposite directions and both require correction
- one completed inventory begins governance; a quarterly practice operates an enterprise

## Sources

- **FP-S0123** [direct · journal article] Niklas Karlsson, George Loewenstein, and Duane Seppi, "The Ostrich Effect: Selective Attention to Information," Journal of Risk and Uncertainty 38, no. 2 (2009): 95-115. Documents that people systematically avoid checking negative financial information to achieve short-term psychological relief, a pattern the researchers confirmed across brokerage account logins and controlled experiments. The chapter's claim that people check their accounts less often as balances worsen describes the predictable output of this avoidance pattern rather than a simple gap in financial literacy.
