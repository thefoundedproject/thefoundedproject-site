# CH42 — Institutions, Power, and Repair

*Part VI: Contributing Agency · Stage: contribution · Draft: Draft 1.3 — Plain-language recheck (abstraction grounding fix) 2026-09-01 (2026-09-01) · 1430 words*

## Thesis (Overview › Core Claim)

Institutions are the durable structural infrastructure of complex society, and their common critique (broken, captured, extractive) is usually accurate but incomplete, because it rarely names what replaces a failing institution, who builds the replacement, or how repair happens in time. Institutional power operates through five mechanisms (formal authority, informal authority, resource allocation, gatekeeping, and institutional narrative), and institutional repair, harder than institutional creation, requires honest diagnosis, cultivated allies, developed alternatives, sustained pressure across years, and the willingness to accept partial outcomes as the realistic ceiling.

## Five-minute summary (Overview text)

**Purpose.** Extends Part VI's contribution architecture from community (CH41) to the institutional scale, where hospitals, schools, courts, regulatory agencies, and civic organizations operate under different dynamics than family or community. The chapter names what institutions are, how power actually moves inside them, and what durable institutional repair requires from a contributor with finite capacity.

**Reader problem.** The reader has spent a decade hearing institutions described as failing, with little guidance on what a single contributor can actually do about it beyond outrage or withdrawal. The chapter gives the reader a map of institutional power and a realistic, multi-year model of repair work, so contribution at this scale doesn't collapse into cynicism or naive idealism.

**Movement.** Five sections: "Why Institutions Get Their Own Chapter" (the incomplete-critique problem) → "What Institutions Actually Are" (structure, function, characteristic failure modes) → "What Power Inside Institutions Actually Does" (five mechanisms) → "What Institutional Repair Requires" (five requirements) → "What the Reader Cannot Do Alone" (finite capacity, ecosystem projects as options).

**Takeaway.** The reader closes the chapter able to read institutional power beyond its formal org chart, understands that institutional repair is realistically a multi-year, partial-outcome project rather than a single intervention, and has named which one or two institutions, inside or outside the Founded ecosystem, actually warrant their finite capacity.

## Key concepts

- what an institution actually is: durable structure that outlasts members, accumulates capability, and enforces standards no individual relationship could
- characteristic institutional failure modes: capture, extraction, ossification, loss of legitimacy, deliberate weakening
- five mechanisms of institutional power: formal authority, informal authority, resource allocation, gatekeeping of access, institutional narrative
- five requirements of institutional repair: honest diagnosis, cultivated allies, developed alternatives, sustained pressure, acceptance of partial outcomes
- the reader's finite capacity: choosing which institutions warrant investment rather than attempting to repair everything

## Sources

- **FP-S0141** [direct · book] Francis Fukuyama, Political Order and Political Decay: From the Industrial Revolution to the Globalization of Democracy (New York: Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2014). Fukuyama identifies two specific mechanisms of institutional decay: institutional rigidity, where structures fail to evolve with changed conditions, and "repatrimonialization," his term for the capture of ostensibly impersonal institutions by powerful elites for their own benefit. The chapter's list of characteristic institutional failures (capture, extraction, ossification, loss of legitimacy, deliberate weakening) tracks Fukuyama's framework closely, applied at the scale of the specific institutions you might actually contribute to rather than at the scale of nation-states.

### FP-CH42-X03 · Q1 · watch

**Proposition.** What could a smart critic say?

**Objection.** A critic could read the chapter as institutionalist apologetics, defending structures (hospitals, universities, courts, regulatory agencies) that some readers believe deserve to collapse rather than be repaired, especially readers who have been personally harmed by a specific institution. The chapter's opening move preempts part of this ("the descriptions are often accurate") but the "What Institutional Repair Requires" section never directly addresses when repair is the wrong call and abandonment or replacement is correct. The chapter gestures at this only obliquely, through "the question of what replaces the institutions if the institutions fail," without naming criteria for when replacement, not repair, is the right response.
