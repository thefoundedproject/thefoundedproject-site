# CH06 — Nature Rejects Absolutes

*Part II: Dynamic Balance · Stage: foundation · Draft: Draft 1.2 — Direct address conversion (second person), 2026-08-30 (2026-08-05) · 1954 words*

## Thesis (Overview › Core Claim)

Healthy systems regulate. Failing systems absolutize. Life at every scale, cellular, personal, relational, civic, runs on the continuous regulated relationship between opposing necessities, not on maximizing any single variable. Nature does not reward absolutes. Nature rejects them, and it has been demonstrating the rejection since the first cells learned to hold an ion gradient across a membrane.

## Five-minute summary (Overview text)

**Purpose.** Opens Part II - Dynamic Balance by establishing the biological floor under everything the book has argued so far. Where Part I diagnosed the broken systems the reader inherited, CH06 grounds the book's answer, dynamic balance, in something older and harder to argue with than theory: the body's own regulation. Every later chapter in Part II (freedom/responsibility, identity/openness, competition/cooperation) inherits this chapter's central claim.

**Reader problem.** The reader has been sold absolutes from every direction, maximum productivity, maximum optimization, maximum self-care, maximum hustle, maximum certainty, maximum tribe, and has likely tried to resolve their own exhaustion by picking harder rather than regulating better. The chapter gives the reader a physiological and philosophical reason to stop choosing a pole and start doing the harder, less glamorous work of regulating the ratio.

**Movement.** Five sections: "What the Body Knows" (the physiological opening, salt/water and sympathetic/parasympathetic regulation) → "What Dynamic Equilibrium Actually Means" (homeostasis reframed as process, allostasis named via Sterling, allostatic load named via McEwen) → "The Older Name for This" (yin/yang, the Ba Gang, the seeds-of-the-opposite teaching, scaled examples across culture, market, relationship, person) → "The Cost of Absolutism" (what breaks when one variable is maximized, the periodization counter-argument answered, scaling to family, workplace, political system, civic culture) → "The Pull Toward Absolutes" and "What This Means For The Reader" (why absolutes feel safe under load, and the hand-off to the next four chapters).

**Takeaway.** The reader closes the chapter believing, on physiological and cross-cultural grounds, that regulation is not a compromise or a moderate half-measure but the actual mechanism of a living system, and that the pull toward absolutes they feel in their own life is an intelligent signal of overload rather than a personal failing.

## Key concepts

- dynamic equilibrium as a continuous process, not a static state (kidneys, breath, insulin, immune self/non-self recognition, autonomic shifts)
- allostasis (Sterling) as a sharper term than homeostasis: the body predicts and adjusts setpoints ahead of demand rather than only correcting after drift
- allostatic load (McEwen) as the name for what breaks down when regulation cannot keep pace with demand
- yin/yang as a three-thousand-year-old parallel tradition naming the same regulated interrelationship, introduced through the Ba Gang (Eight Principles) diagnostic framework from Dr. Thompson's Chinese Medicine training
- the seeds-of-the-opposite teaching: every extreme generates the conditions for its own reversal from inside itself (Lao Tzu, Tao Te Ching ch. 40), demonstrated at the scale of culture, market, relationship, and person
- the pull toward absolutes as an overloaded nervous system's last available move, not a character flaw
- periodization as dynamic balance with a stopwatch, the chapter's answer to the elite-athlete counter-argument

Different studies select different biomarkers and scoring methods. Towards a consensus definition of allostatic load — multi-cohort meta-analysis. **Response.** mild. The chapter does NOT cite AL by name; it uses the broader concept of cumulative regulation failure. The CONSTRUCT-validity critique doesn't undermine the PHENOMENON (cumulative stress damage is well established); it undermines a specific measurement scheme.

**Revision.** if AL gets named explicitly in revision (it doesn't currently), add a one-sentence acknowledgment that the measurement model is still being refined while the underlying biology is well documented.

### FP-CH06-X04 · EDGE-1 · revision-suggested

**Proposition.** Allostasis says the body REGULATES TOWARD CHANGE, not toward constancy

**Objection.** Sterling's allostasis framework (sources above) explicitly argues that homeostasis as taught (constancy, error-correction) is wrong for many real physiological systems. The chapter's deeper claim (that life runs on regulated relationships between opposing necessities, not absolutes) is consistent with BOTH frameworks, but using "allostasis" in some places would strengthen the predictive-regulation argument and align the chapter with the most current physiology.
