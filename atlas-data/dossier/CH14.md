# CH14 — The Moral Boundary Principle

*Part II: Dynamic Balance · Stage: foundation · Draft: Draft 1.3 - Plain-language recheck (CH01 reader test) 2026-09-01 (2026-08-05) · 1832 words*

## Thesis (Overview › Core Claim)

The Dynamic Balance Principle applies to necessary competing forces, not to inherently immoral acts. Some apparent tensions are not tensions. They are violations dressed in the language of competing forces, and a theory that cannot tell the difference is not a theory worth defending.

## Five-minute summary (Overview text)

**Purpose.** Closes Part II - Dynamic Balance by drawing the floor underneath every tension pair the part has walked. CH11, CH12, and CH13 taught the reader to regulate real tensions. CH14 teaches the reader what is not a tension at all. See Draft for the developed argument.

**Reader problem.** The reader has just spent three chapters learning to regulate real tensions: freedom and responsibility, identity and openness, security and adaptability. That skill is dangerous without a floor. A reader who has only learned "everything is a tension to be balanced" is a reader who can be talked into balancing their own dignity against someone else's interest in violating it. CH14 gives the reader the tool to refuse that move.

**Movement.** Five sections: why Part II needs a floor, what the principle does, what sits outside the field, how the reader uses the principle, what this means for the rest of the book. See the full structure in CH14 - Draft.

**Takeaway.** The reader closes Part II with both a diagnostic floor (Part I) and a philosophical floor (Part II): the recognition that some things are not tensions to regulate, they are violations to refuse, and no amount of balance-language changes that.

## Key concepts

- the distinction between real tensions (two necessary forces) and false tensions (one necessary force versus a violation)
- the list of acts that sit outside the field of Dynamic Balance: slavery, genocide, torture, child abuse, intentional cruelty, deliberate disinformation, exploitation for exploitation's sake, suffering for suffering's sake
- the two diagnostic questions: are both sides necessary for flourishing, and who benefits from the framing
- moral relativism's "anything goes" failure as the philosophical ancestor of the chapter's argument
- balance-language misuse by people in power (corporate, political, religious, clinical) as the threat the principle guards against

## Challenge records

### FP-CH14-X01 · COUNTER-1 · revision-suggested

**Proposition.** Cultural relativism has legitimate uses in anthropology and cross-cultural ethics

**Objection.** Not all relativism is bad-faith. Anthropological relativism as a METHODOLOGICAL tool (suspending judgment to understand a culture on its own terms before evaluating it) is different from relativism as a MORAL CONCLUSION (there is no fact of the matter about whether slavery is wrong).

**Response.** worth a light touch. The chapter's targets (corporate leaders invoking balance to defend extraction, political actors both-sidesing a one-sided question) are clearly moral-relativism-as-conclusion, not methodological anthropological relativism.

**Revision.** none required — the chapter's examples are specific enough that this distinction is implicitly preserved. But if Stephen wants to preempt a sophisticated reader's objection, one clause distinguishing "trying to understand why a culture believes X" from "concluding X is therefore not wrong" would help.

**Remaining uncertainty.** The Research Map suggests a revision that has not been confirmed in the draft.

### FP-CH14-X02 · EDGE-1 · no-change-needed

**Proposition.** Determining WHICH acts belong on the "outside the field" list is itself contested

**Objection.** The chapter's list (slavery, genocide, torture, child abuse, etc.) draws broad consensus, but edge cases exist (e.g., is capital punishment a violation? Is factory farming?) where reasonable people disagree about which side of the moral boundary an act falls on.

**Response.** the chapter already handles this. "The list could be longer. The list could be argued" explicitly acknowledges contestability at the margins while holding firm on the clear cases. This is intellectually honest and doesn't need further hedging.

### FP-CH14-X03 · Q1 · watch

**Proposition.** What could a smart critic say?

**Objection.** A critic could say the chapter just relocates the relativism problem one level down. Who decided slavery, genocide, torture, and the rest belong on the "outside the field" list, and by what authority does that list bind the reader? The chapter answers this partially by grounding the move in the standard philosophical objection to relativism (SEP, the AAA 1947 statement) rather than personal assertion, and by naming the list explicit-and-illustrative rather than exhaustive ("The list could be longer. The list could be argued."). That is a real defense, not a dodge. But the chapter never states the actual test with the same precision it applies to naming examples: "an act is a violation when the act takes from a person, community, or environment what is required for flourishing, and offers nothing in return that the recipient would accept under conditions of full agency and information." A sharp critic will press on that test's edges (capital punishment, factory farming, war) faster than the chapter invites. EDGE-1 in the Research Map already flags this and judges the chapter's current hedge sufficient. Watch this on Stephen's pass — it's the chapter's single load-bearing sentence.

**Remaining uncertainty.** A risk to watch; no decision required yet.
