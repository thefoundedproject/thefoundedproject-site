# CH28 — Values Under Stress

*Part IV: Governing Agency · Stage: governance · Draft: Draft 1.2 — Plain-language recheck (CH01 reader test) 2026-09-01 (2026-09-01) · 1910 words*

## Thesis (Overview › Core Claim)

The values a reader states on a quiet afternoon are not the values that govern the reader's decisions under stress. Robust values get built deliberately, through four practices, not discovered by producing a better list.

## Five-minute summary (Overview text)

**Purpose.** Moves Part IV - Governing Agency from structure (bylaws, boards, decision memos live in adjacent chapters) into the material those structures are built to protect: the reader's actual values. The chapter names the gap between the values a reader states in calm conditions and the values that actually govern decisions under stress, then walks the four practices that close the gap. It sets up the next chapter's decision memos and personal bylaws as the operational extension of this work.

**Reader problem.** The reader has a list of values they'd give without hesitation, honesty, family, health, service, and a pattern of decisions under pressure that doesn't match the list. The chapter gives the reader the mechanism for the gap and a concrete path to closing it, instead of leaving the reader to read the gap as a character flaw.

**Movement.** Five sections: "The Values Most People Articulate" (names the stated list and the gap) → "What Stress Does to Values" (the mechanism, grounded in the hot-cold empathy gap) → "How to Build Robust Values" (the four practices) → "What the Values Look Like in Operation" (five scenes of a value doing the work stress would otherwise have done) → "What Values Cannot Do" (the honest limits). Closes with a hand-off to the next chapter's decision memos and personal bylaws.

**Takeaway.** The reader closes the chapter with a name for why their stated values and their stress-decisions diverge, and with four concrete, buildable practices for closing the gap, rather than a renewed resolution to simply try harder next time.

## Key concepts

- the stated-values list versus the operational values that govern decisions under stress
- the hot-cold empathy gap: a calm state cannot accurately predict behavior in a stressed state
- the gap as evidence, not moral failure
- four practices for building robust values: honest naming, values revision, conditional protection, regular review
- values that operate without requiring willpower in the moment
- what values can and cannot do (they don't make decisions easy, they don't replace conditions, they don't prevent every violation, they don't install quickly)

## Sources

- **FP-S0126** [direct · journal article] George Loewenstein, "Hot-Cold Empathy Gaps and Medical Decision Making," Health Psychology 24, no. 4 (2005): S49-S56. Loewenstein's research on the hot-cold empathy gap shows that people in a calm ("cold") state systematically underestimate how much a "hot," aroused, or stressed state will change their preferences and behavior once they are actually in it. This is the mechanism behind the chapter's central claim: the values a reader states on "a quiet Sunday afternoon" are cold-state values, and the decision made under actual pressure is generated by a different, hot-state process that the cold-state self cannot fully predict or bind in advance.

### FP-CH28-X01 · COUNTER-1 · no-change-needed

**Proposition.** The hot-cold empathy gap research is primarily about predicting one's own future behavior under different visceral states (hunger, pain, arousal) — the chapter extends it to values under general life stress (deadlines, overwhelm, conflict), which is a broader and less visceral category of "hot" state than the original research's focus

**Objection.** The hot-cold empathy gap research is primarily about predicting one's own future behavior under different visceral states (hunger, pain, arousal) — the chapter extends it to values under general life stress (deadlines, overwhelm, conflict), which is a broader and less visceral category of "hot" state than the original research's focus

**Response.** no adjustment needed. The underlying mechanism (cold-state prediction failing to anticipate hot-state behavior) generalizes reasonably to stress broadly, and the chapter's claim is about the general phenomenon rather than a specific visceral trigger. The extension is consistent with how the concept has been applied in subsequent literature beyond Loewenstein's original medical-decision context.

### FP-CH28-X03 · Q1 · watch

**Proposition.** What could a smart critic say?

**Objection.** The chapter reads, section by section, as a values-improvement checklist: name the gap, revise, protect, review. A critic could call this self-help formula dressed in Founded language, especially in "What the Values Look Like in Operation," where all five scenes resolve cleanly (the reader declines, discloses, declines again, has the conversation, declines a third time). No scene shows the practice failing or costing more than the chapter admits. The chapter's own "What Values Cannot Do" section is the honest answer to this critique, but it arrives after five consecutive success scenes have already set the reader's expectations.

**Remaining uncertainty.** A risk to watch; no decision required yet.

### FP-CH28-X04 · Q6 · watch

**Proposition.** Respects structural constraints?

**Objection.** The chapter is almost entirely internal: naming, revision, protection, and review are all things the reader does alone. It gestures at structural constraint once ("A reader who values rest cannot rest inside conditions that do not allow rest") but does not develop what happens when the reader's actual conditions, an unsupportive workplace, a partner who does not share the value, poverty, make the four practices materially harder to execute for some readers than others.

**Remaining uncertainty.** A risk to watch; no decision required yet.
